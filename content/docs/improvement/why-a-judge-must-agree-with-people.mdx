description: Why a station's judge stays hidden until its scores match people's, how that agreement is measured, and why grading never stops work.

A station's [judge](/docs/reference/glossary#judge) is a model that reads a
share of the station's runs and scores each one against a rubric or a list of
claims the team wrote. It can read every sampled run, which no person has time to do, and so
it can say how a station is doing between the runs people look at closely. A
model can also misread a rubric, or read it more strictly or loosely than the
team meant. A score that disagrees with what people would have said is worse
than no score, because it looks like a measurement.

So a judge starts out hidden. Its scores and its reasons show only once
Spotlight has measured their agreement with people's, and the agreement
has reached the minimum agreement the station sets in `min_agreement`.

## Where people's scores come from

Agreement needs runs that both the judge and people scored. People score runs
in two ways.

The first is a grade. One in five judged runs is put in the inbox to be graded,
with the run's last words and its changes. The item does not show the judge's
score. Anyone on the team, or an owner or admin of the workspace, can grade
it.

The second needs no extra work. On a yes-or-no rubric, what people already do
with a run says whether it was good: accepting the run, by moving the task to the completed station after it, is a yes,
sending it back is a no, and failing those, a merged pull request is a yes and
one closed unmerged is a no. Answering an approval counts as neither: it is
the run asking a person, and a run that only paused for an approval would
otherwise count as accepted. A 1 to 5 rubric has no such
signal, because closing a task says nothing about whether the run was a 3 or a
4, so it relies on grades alone. So do claims: closing a task says the run was
good enough, not which of its claims were true.

In the docs' view, the second source is what makes a judge usable on a busy
station: its agreement can be measured from the work people do anyway, and
grades fill the gaps.

## How agreement is measured

For a yes-or-no rubric, Spotlight uses Cohen's kappa rather than the share of
runs where both said the same. If most of a station's runs are good, a judge
that says yes to everything agrees with people most of the time while telling
them nothing. Kappa counts only the agreement beyond what chance would give,
so such a judge scores near zero.

For a 1 to 5 rubric, Spotlight uses Spearman's rank correlation: whether the
runs the judge ranks higher are the ones people rank higher. A judge that
scores every run one point lower than people do still ranks them the same way,
and its scores still tell good runs from bad. The mean absolute error, shown
beside it, says how far apart the two scores sit.

For claims, Spotlight sets each claim's result from the judge beside the same
claim's result from people, pools those pairs across every claim, and measures
Cohen's kappa over them. It also measures each claim on its own. A judge can
read most claims as people do and one of them differently; the pooled figure
says whether the judge is qualified, and the per-claim figures say which
claim to reword.

At least 10 runs need both scores before any measure counts. In the docs'
view, with fewer, one or two runs would decide the result.

## Claims instead of one score

A rubric asks the judge for one score of the whole run, and a score from 1 to
5 drifts from one call to the next. A claim such as "the change adds a test for
the reported bug" is narrower: the judge answers it yes or no and cites what
shows it, and a person can check that one answer and argue with it. Claims
make a judge steadier and easier to check against people, which is why a
station can have them. In the docs' view, a person who disagrees with a 3 has
little to point at, while a person who disagrees with one claim can say which.

A claims judge scores a run as the share of its claims met. Whether that share
is enough to pass matters only when a replay decides whether a run succeeded,
so the team sets it beside the claims as `pass_threshold`, every claim unless told
otherwise. A rubric can stay beside the claims, to tell the judge what the
station is for.

## Each rubric starts again

Agreement is kept per rubric version. A rubric version covers the rubric, the
claims and the pass threshold together. Changing any of them changes what
the judge is asked, so the agreement measured under the old text says nothing
about the new one, and the judge is hidden again until it has agreed with
people under the new text. A changed rubric is also a changed station config,
so its runs start a new group under "Runs by config" on Insights, like any
other change. See
[Why each run records its station's config](/docs/improvement/why-each-run-records-its-stations-config).

## Reasons a person can check

Each of the judge's reasons cites the steps of the run or the hunks of its
changes, and a reason that cites something the judge did not read
is dropped. A person looking at a score can follow each reason to what the
judge saw, and judge it for themselves. While the judge is hidden, its reasons
are hidden with its score. In the docs' view, a reason from a judge that is not qualified
would carry the same weight to a reader as the score it explains.

## A judge never decides

The judge is told that its verdict is an early signal for the team, never a
decision. Nothing is approved, moved or stopped by a score, and nothing
waits on a grade: a task moves on as it would without either. Whether a run
was good enough is still decided by the people who answer the inbox.
