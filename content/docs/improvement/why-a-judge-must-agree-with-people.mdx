title: Why a judge must agree with people before its verdicts show

---

A station's [judge](/docs/reference/glossary#judge) is a model that reads a
share of the station's runs and checks each one against a list of claims the
team wrote. It can read every sampled run, which no person has time to do, and
so it can say how a station is doing between the runs people look at closely.
A model can also misread a claim, or read it more strictly or loosely than the
team meant. A verdict that disagrees with what people would have said is worse
than no verdict, because it looks like a measurement.

So a judge starts out hidden. Its verdicts and its reasons show only once
Spotlight has measured their agreement with people's, and the agreement
has reached the minimum agreement the station sets in `min_agreement`.

## Why the judge checks claims

A claim such as "the change adds a test for the reported bug" is narrow. The
judge answers it yes or no and cites what shows it, and a person can check
that one answer and argue with it. In the docs' view, a person who disagrees
with a score of the whole run has little to point at, while a person who
disagrees with one claim can say which.

The claims also give one verdict. A run passes when it meets at least the share
of its claims that the station sets in `pass_threshold`, every claim unless told
otherwise. People give the same kind of answer when they act on a run: they
accept it or send it back. Because the two answers have the same shape, what
people already do with a run can check the judge.

## Where people's verdicts come from

Agreement needs runs that both the judge and people gave a verdict on.
People's verdict needs no extra work. What people already do with a run says
whether it was good: accepting the run, by moving the task to the completed station
after it, is a pass, and sending it back is a fail. Failing those, a merged
pull request is a pass for the station's last completed run on the task, the
run whose work the merge took. A pull request closed without a merge is no
verdict: it can close because the feature is no longer wanted, or because
another pull request took its place. Answering an approval
counts as neither: it is the run asking a person, and a run that only paused
for an approval would otherwise count as accepted.

Spotlight never asks a person for a verdict on a run. To check the judge,
such a verdict would have to mark each of the station's claims, and a station
can have many, on a run the person already acted on. The cost is that a
station whose runs people rarely accept, send back or merge gets few runs
with both verdicts, so its judge can stay hidden longer.

## How agreement is measured

Spotlight uses Cohen's kappa on the two verdicts, rather than the share of runs
where both said the same. If most of a station's runs are good, a judge that
passes everything agrees with people most of the time while telling them
nothing. Kappa counts only the agreement beyond what chance would give, so
such a judge gets a kappa near zero.

At least 10 runs need both verdicts before the measure counts. In the docs'
view, with fewer, one or two runs would decide the result.

## Each claims version starts again

Agreement is kept per claims version. A claims version covers the claims and
the pass threshold together. Changing either changes what the judge is asked,
so the agreement measured under the old claims says nothing about the new ones,
and the judge is hidden again until it has agreed with people under the new
claims.

To keep that wait short, Spotlight judges past runs again under the new
claims, as soon as the line changes: up to 50 recent runs that already have
people's verdict from an accept, a send-back or a merge. So the new claims
can be measured without waiting for new runs. See
[Re-judging after a claims change](/docs/reference/app/tasks-runs-and-the-inbox#re-judging-after-a-claims-change).

A changed judge is also a changed station config, so it shows under
"Config changes" on the Insights Stations tab, like any other change. See
[Why each run records its station's config](/docs/improvement/why-each-run-records-its-stations-config).

## Reasons a person can check

Each of the judge's reasons cites the steps of the run or the hunks of its
changes, and a reason that cites something the judge did not read
is dropped. A person looking at a score can follow each reason to what the
judge saw, and judge it for themselves. While the judge is hidden, its reasons
are hidden with its score. In the docs' view, a reason from a judge that is not qualified
would carry the same weight to a reader as the score it explains.

## When the judge and people disagree

A run where the judge's verdict and people's differ is the best evidence of
what the claims get wrong. Spotlight puts such a run in the inbox as a
[judge disagreement](/docs/reference/app/tasks-runs-and-the-inbox#judge-disagreements),
for the person whose action disagreed with the judge. It is the only time
Spotlight asks a person about the judge, and it asks only about the claims in
dispute.
Each claim in dispute gets its own answer and its own reason: the person
says whether the judge was right or the claim is wrong for this run, and why.
Where no claim is in dispute, because the judge passed the run, the person
says what was wrong, and which claim should have caught it, if any. A claims
revision learns from a disagreement only once each claim in dispute has an
answer.

When people's verdict changes so that it agrees with the judge's, or is gone,
or the claims change, Spotlight withdraws the question, and an answer it already has
stops counting. In the docs' view, an answer is worth something only while it
describes a disagreement under the claims the judge uses.

An answer never changes the run's verdicts or the judge's agreement with
people. In the docs' view, a person who is asked "was the judge wrong?" has
already seen the judge's verdict, so the answer is not an independent verdict
like an accept or a send-back. Counting it would let the judge's verdict lean on
the measure of its own agreement.

The answers drive a
[claims revision](/docs/reference/app/tasks-runs-and-the-inbox#claims-revisions)
instead. When people say often enough that one claim is wrong, or name
problems no claim checks, or when the judge's agreement falls below
`min_agreement`, Spotlight drafts new claims and judges past labelled runs
again with them, from what those runs stored. It picks the new claims on
some runs and checks them on runs it held out. In the docs' view, claims that
only fit the runs they were picked on would agree with people there and
nowhere else, the same overfitting a
[search holds tasks out](/docs/improvement/why-a-search-holds-tasks-out)
for. When the new claims agree with people better on the held-out runs,
Spotlight opens a pull request that changes only the claims. A person still
decides by merging it.

While the judge does not agree with people well enough, Spotlight also does
not search for a better config of the station, and a
[replay check](/docs/reference/api/replay-checks#results) of a planned change
fails. Both score configs with the judge, and in the docs' view a score from a
judge that people do not trust cannot tell a better config from a worse one.

## A judge never decides a live run

The judge is told that its verdict is an early signal for the team, never a
decision. Nothing is approved, moved or stopped by a score, and nothing
waits on a judge disagreement: a task moves on as it would without either. Whether a run
was good enough is still decided by the people who answer the inbox.
